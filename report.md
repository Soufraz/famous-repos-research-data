# How much of GitHub's most-starred AI tooling is written by coding agents?

**AI authorship, churn and self-published results in 52 of GitHub's most-starred AI-tooling repositories**

Rafael Frazao · data as of 2026-11-08 · [interactive version](https://www.soufraz.com/famous-repos-research/) · [data and evidence](https://github.com/Soufraz/famous-repos-research-data)

## Summary

We audited the full git history of 52 of the most-starred repositories in GitHub's AI-tooling topics (324,088 authored commits). 38 carry explicit AI signatures on at least 5% of their commits (all-time or in their last 180 days of activity), and 9 more commit instructions for coding agents (CLAUDE.md, AGENTS.md and similar) while signing fewer than 5%. Only 5 show neither signal, which means *unknown*, not *no AI*. In the median repository, 16.9% of the commits in its last 180 days of activity are AI-signed, a lower bound; 21 of 52 are at 25% or more. How much AI a repository uses does not predict how famous it is (Spearman ρ = -0.05, p = 0.72). Compared with 12 famous pre-Copilot projects at the same age (six months), the cohort labels 2.0 times as many of its commits as fixes (median 34.1% vs 17.3%) yet keeps more of the code it writes (median survival 67.1% vs 41.7%). The fix share, churn and survival gaps are concentrated in the 31 projects started in 2025 or later: among them, repositories that sign at least 5% of their commits and those that sign less show no detectable difference, and the 10 cohort projects started earlier do not differ detectably from the baseline. Heavy AI use is concentrated in the newest projects (AI share vs start date: ρ = 0.72), so this sample cannot separate AI use from recency. At one year (20 cohort projects old enough), the fix share gap remains but the churn and survival gaps are no longer detectable. 15 repositories publish outcome benchmarks with a runnable harness and committed results, 11 of them AI-signed, and several publish results that did not go their way.

The claim is descriptive. Heavy AI use, frequent fixing and published evidence of results coexist in today's famous AI-tooling projects. This does not show that AI use causes popularity or quality, and the published results are the authors' own, not re-run here.

## 1. What was measured

- **Cohort (52 repositories).** The top 10 by stars in each of 7 GitHub topics (`agent-harness`, `ai-agents`, `coding-agents`, `context-engineering`, `llm`, `prompt-engineering`, `spec-driven-development`) as listed on 2026-11-08, minus repositories that are reading material or collections rather than tools (books, courses, guides, prompt collections, awesome-lists), plus the 4 repositories the study started from (OmniRoute, context-mode, rtk, superpowers). No other repository was left out, whatever its age or size. 39 of the 52 were created in 2025 or later; 11 before 2024 (AutoGPT, anything-llm, dify, go-zero, langchain, lobehub, ollama, open-webui, ragflow, transformers, yao). Half of them have between 15,632 and 126,250 stars (median 65,126), fetched from the GitHub API on 2026-11-08.
- **Baseline (12 repositories).** Famous projects (over 40k stars) whose first year ended before GitHub Copilot became generally available (2022-06-21): denoland/deno, excalidraw/excalidraw, fastapi/fastapi, junegunn/fzf, gin-gonic/gin, gradio-app/gradio, hoppscotch/hoppscotch, jesseduffield/lazygit, n8n-io/n8n, microsoft/playwright, BurntSushi/ripgrep, huggingface/transformers. Both groups are chosen by today's fame. Cohort snapshots taken before that date (go-zero, transformers, yao at day 180) are left out of the age comparison, so no repository counts on both sides.
- **Commits.** Full history of the default branch; merge commits (38,229) and maintenance bots such as dependabot and release bots (10,346) are left out of every percentage. get-shit-done and gsd-core share 2,554 commits of history (up to 2026-05-22), which count in both.
- **AI signals.** (1) *Signed*: a commit carries an AI co-author trailer, an AI agent as author or committer, or a tool's "Generated with" footer, matched on the identities the tools write. (2) *Configured*: the repository commits agent instructions (`CLAUDE.md`, `AGENTS.md`, `.cursor/`, `.github/copilot-instructions.md`, ...) or runs a coding agent in CI.
- **Fix %.** Share of commits whose subject contains fix, bug, hotfix or regression. **Churn ratio**: lines added plus deleted over all history, divided by lines at HEAD. **Survival %**: lines at HEAD divided by lines ever added (source code and Markdown; vendored, generated, translated, lock and changelog files excluded).
- **Published results.** Each repository's own claims about its performance, located by file and line in the audited commit and classified by evidence type. No benchmark was re-run.

### Unsigned does not mean "no AI"

Every AI percentage here is a lower bound. Agents can be configured not to add attribution, squash merges can drop trailers, and autocomplete or pasted chat output never signs anything. Claude Code adds a trailer by default, which is partly why Claude accounts for 85.3% of the 36,892 signed commits; tools that sign less often are under-counted. Detecting agent commits without signatures is close to guesswork ([Agent or Human?, 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.36229)), so we do not try.

## 4. Fixing and churn at the same age

At HEAD the cohort's median fix share is 36.3% (interquartile range 27.6%-47.2%), its median churn ratio 2.08x and its median survival 65%.

Churn depends on a project's age, so the comparison with the baseline is made at the same age: each repository is checked out at its last mainline commit 180 and 365 days after its first commit and measured the same way.

| Metric (median) | Cohort, day 180 | Baseline, day 180 | | Cohort, day 365 | Baseline, day 365 | |
| --- | ---: | ---: | --- | ---: | ---: | --- |
| Commits | 1318 | 678.5 | p = 0.082 | 2013.5 | 1119.5 | p = 0.045 |
| Fix % | 34.1 | 17.3 | p < 0.001 | 27.3 | 17.1 | p < 0.001 |
| Churn ratio | 1.92 | 3.77 | p = 0.008 | 2.47 | 3.56 | p = 0.41 |
| Survival % | 67.1 | 41.7 | p = 0.011 | 57.55 | 43.65 | p = 0.46 |
| Repositories | 41 | 12 | | 20 | 12 | |

p-values: two-sided Mann-Whitney U, 10,000 permutations, not corrected for the number of tests. Baseline repositories show 0% AI-signed commits at both ages, as they should, which is a check on the detector's false-positive rate. At day 180, 41 of the 52 cohort repositories are compared: 8 have less than 180 days of history (deepseek-harness, elephant-goldfish-model, get-shit-done, gsd-2, holaOS, munder-difflin, ponytail, thinkrail) and 3 snapshots predate Copilot. Day 180 is the main comparison: at day 365 only 20 cohort repositories qualify, and they were far less AI-heavy at that age (median 0.8% AI-signed).

The comparison is between eras (2022-2026 projects vs 2013-2020 projects), not between AI and no AI. Within the cohort, AI share tracks fix share (Spearman ρ = 0.46 at day 180, p = 0.003; ρ = 0.34 at HEAD, p = 0.017) but not churn (Spearman ρ = -0.22 at day 180, p = 0.17; ρ = -0.23 at HEAD, p = 0.096) or survival (Spearman ρ = 0.21 at day 180, p = 0.18; ρ = 0.24 at HEAD, p = 0.087). But AI share also rises with how recently a project started (ρ = 0.72, p < 0.001): 23 of the 31 projects started in 2025 or later signed at least 5% of their commits by day 180, against 0 of the 10 started earlier. Splitting by start year:

| Metric (median, day 180) | Started 2025+, signed ≥5% | Started 2025+, signed <5% | Started 2022-2024 | Baseline | 2025+: signed vs not | 2025+ vs baseline | 2022-2024 vs baseline |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | --- | --- | --- |
| Fix share | 36.2 | 34.65 | 22 | 17.3 | p = 0.47 | p < 0.001 | p = 0.18 |
| Churn | 1.89 | 1.86 | 2.79 | 3.77 | p = 0.57 | p = 0.003 | p = 0.37 |
| Survival | 69.2 | 69.25 | 52.95 | 41.7 | p = 0.60 | p = 0.003 | p = 0.38 |
| Repositories | 23 | 8 | 10 | 12 | | | |

In short: the fix share, churn and survival gaps are concentrated in the 31 projects started in 2025 or later: among them, repositories that sign at least 5% of their commits and those that sign less show no detectable difference, and the 10 cohort projects started earlier do not differ detectably from the baseline. Heavy AI use is concentrated in the newest projects (AI share vs start date: ρ = 0.72), so this sample cannot separate AI use from recency. A less-signed project is not necessarily one without agents (unsigned is unknown, not no AI), so the comparison inside the newest projects is between more and less visible AI use.

Two cautions. Fix % is read from commit subjects, and recent projects write conventional subjects ("fix: ...") far more consistently than people did in 2013-2020, so part of the gap may be writing style rather than more bugs. And churn counts every committed line: vendored or generated files committed under ordinary paths (gradio's copy of `bootstrap.js`, playwright's generated protocol files) inflate churn in either group, which is why medians are reported. What the data supports: these projects label many commits as fixes, and they do not discard more of their code than famous projects did at the same age.

## 5. What they publish about their own results

Strongest evidence each repository publishes about its own performance: harness + committed results: 24; harness, no committed results: 12; results, no harness: 8; claim without data: 3; no quantitative results: 3; third-party only: 2. Of the 24 with a harness and committed results, 15 measure whether the tool does its job (tokens saved, accuracy, cost, task success) and 9 measure only the speed of the software itself (AutoGPT, Paradev, agent-orchestrator, cmux, deepseek-harness, happy, orca, transformers, yao).

## 6. What this does not show

- **No causation.** Famous projects were selected by today's stars, so they are survivors. Projects that used AI the same way and failed are not in the sample.
- **AI-tooling projects, not famous projects in general.** People who build tools for coding agents are the likeliest to use them, so these shares say nothing about famous repositories in other domains.
- **A snapshot of moving lists.** Topic rankings change daily; the cohort is the top 10 of each topic on 2026-11-08.
- **AI share is a lower bound.** Signatures measure attribution, not authorship, and Claude Code is over-represented because it signs by default.
- **Agent configuration can be product, not process.** Only root-level paths are matched, but in AI-tooling repositories a root `CLAUDE.md`, `.claude/` or `.cursor/` can be a template the product ships rather than instructions for the project's own development.
- **Signed is not unreviewed.** A signature shows a coding agent wrote or co-wrote a commit; it says nothing about how carefully a person reviewed it.
- **AI use and recency go together.** Almost all heavy AI use is in projects started in 2025 or later, so differences from older projects cannot be attributed to AI rather than to when a project started, or to the tools and conventions of its time.
- **Stars are a noisy popularity measure** ([Six Million (Suspected) Fake Stars, ICSE 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.13459)).
- **Fix % is keyword-based** and sensitive to commit-message conventions; it is not a defect rate.
- **Churn mixes everything that is committed**, including vendored or generated files that escape the exclusion rules.
- **Published results are self-reported.** They were located and classified, not reproduced; the classification of outcome vs engineering benchmarks is manual.
- **Small baseline.** 12 pre-Copilot projects, chosen by fame, language and clone size.

## 7. Related work

**AI adoption and code quality**

- [Speed at the Cost of Quality: How Cursor AI Increases Short-Term Velocity and Long-Term Complexity in Open-Source Projects](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.04427), He, Miller, Agarwal, Kästner, Vasilescu (Carnegie Mellon), MSR 2026. Compares GitHub projects that adopted Cursor (first commit touching Cursor configuration files such as .cursorrules) with matched non-adopters. Lines added rose 3-5x in the first month and faded after two; static-analysis warnings (+30%) and code complexity (+41%) rose persistently and predicted later slowdowns. *Relation to this study:* The closest study design. It measures the average effect of adoption; we describe the most-starred projects, which are survivors by construction. Its use of agent config files as an adoption signal is the same idea as our agent-config signal.
- [AI Copilot Code Quality: 2025 Look Back at 12 Months of Data (and the 2024 'Coding on Copilot' report)](https://www.gitclear.com/ai_assistant_code_quality_2025_research), GitClear, Industry report, 2024-2025. 211M changed lines (2020-2024): a spike in duplicated code blocks (copy/pasted lines rose from 8.3% to 12.3% of changed lines between 2021 and 2024 and exceeded moved lines for the first time), more short-term churn, and refactoring-associated lines falling from 25% of changed lines in 2021 to under 10% in 2024. Not peer reviewed; GitClear sells code-analysis tooling. *Relation to this study:* GitClear's churn means lines revised within two weeks; ours is lifetime churn relative to the code at HEAD. Both find churn rising with AI; neither measures whether the software works.
- [Debt Behind the AI Boom: A Large-Scale Empirical Study of AI-Generated Code in the Wild](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.28592), Liu, Widyasari, Zhao, Irsan, Chen, Lo, arXiv 2603.28592, 2026. 302.6k AI-authored commits from 6,299 repositories and five assistants; runs static analysis before and after each commit and tracks introduced issues to HEAD. *Relation to this study:* Uses the same kind of commit attribution. It asks what defects AI commits introduce; we ask how heavily famous projects use AI and what results they publish.
- [To What Extent Does Agent-generated Code Require Maintenance? An Empirical Study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.06464), Sawada, Shirai, Kashiwa, Yamaguchi, Iwata, Iida, EASE 2026 (arXiv 2605.06464). Compares the later maintenance of 508 AI-generated and 508 human-written files across 100 repositories. *Relation to this study:* File-level maintenance comparison; our churn and survival metrics are repository-level and do not separate AI-written lines from human-written ones.

**Measuring agent activity on GitHub**

- [On the Use of Agentic Coding: An Empirical Study of Pull Requests on GitHub](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.14745), Watanabe, Li, Kashiwa, Reid, Iida, Hassan, ACM TOSEM (accepted); arXiv 2509.14745, 2025. 567 Claude Code pull requests across 157 projects; 34.1% of the commits made while revising them were co-authored by Claude. *Relation to this study:* Finds Claude Code PRs by the "Generated with Claude Code" description marker and measures AI co-authorship of revision commits with the same "Co-Authored-By: Claude" trailer that dominates our AI-signed counts.
- [How Do AI Coding Agents Contribute to Software Development? An Empirical Study of Agentic Pull Requests](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.21832), Mazloomzadeh, Morovati, Khomh, arXiv 2607.21832, 2026. Compares pull requests from OpenAI Codex, GitHub Copilot, Cursor, Devin and Claude Code (AIDev dataset) with human PRs in Python repositories with more than 100 stars, over time. Agents are recognised by branch prefixes (codex/, copilot/, cursor/), the Devin bot account, and "Co-Authored-By: Claude" in the PR body. *Relation to this study:* Pull-request level view of the same tools whose signatures we count at commit level.
- [Detecting AI Coding Agents in Open Source: A Validated Multi-Method Census of 180 Million Repositories](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.24429), Khosravani, Mockus, arXiv 2606.24429, 2026. Multi-method detection (configuration-file scanning, commit-message analysis, author-identity matching, bot-signature lookup) across World of Code's 180M+ Git repositories. Bot-account lookup alone recovers only 3.3% of the 850,157 Claude Code commits found in one snapshot, so single-signal estimates are biased low. *Relation to this study:* Ecosystem-scale detection that already combines commit signatures with agent-config files; we apply the same two kinds of signal to the cohort.
- [Agent or Human? Detecting Commits by AI Coding Agents in the Absence of Authorship Signatures](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.36229), Horikawa, Li, Kashiwa, Ouatiti, Sayagh, Adams, Iida, Hassan, arXiv 2610.36229, 2026. With authorship signatures masked, detectors reach ROC AUC up to 0.859, but the best one has a precision of only 0.332 at the observed agent share, and detection transfers poorly to held-out agents (TF-IDF AUC only 0.524 for Claude Code). The authors conclude signature-based labels are a lower bound on agent activity. *Relation to this study:* Why an unsigned commit cannot be reliably attributed from its style, and why we report signed AI as a lower bound instead of guessing.

**Productivity**

- [Measuring the Impact of Early-2025 AI on Experienced Open-Source Developer Productivity](https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/), METR, arXiv 2507.09089, 2025. Randomized trial: 16 experienced developers, 246 tasks in their own mature repositories. With early-2025 AI tools (mainly Cursor with Claude 3.5/3.7 Sonnet) tasks took 19% longer, while developers believed they were about 20% faster. *Relation to this study:* Measures individual speed on mature codebases; our cohort is mostly young projects and we do not measure speed.
- [2025 State of AI-assisted Software Development (DORA)](https://dora.dev/dora-report-2025/), Google Cloud DORA, Industry survey, 2025. Survey of nearly 5,000 professionals: AI acts as an amplifier of existing team strengths and weaknesses; adoption is now linked to higher throughput but still to lower delivery stability. *Relation to this study:* Practice-level counterpart to our observation that some AI-heavy projects pair fast iteration with their own evaluation harnesses.

**Vibe coding**

- [Vibe Coding in Practice: Motivations, Challenges, and a Future Outlook - a Grey Literature Review](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.00328), Fawzy, Tahir, Blincoe, ICSE 2026 (SEIP). 101 practitioner sources (518 firsthand accounts): vibe coders are motivated by speed and accessibility, most perceive the resulting code as fast but flawed, and QA practices are frequently overlooked. *Relation to this study:* Defines the practice qualitatively; we look for its footprint in the git history of famous projects.
- [Evaluation-Driven Development and Operations of LLM Agents: A Process Model and Reference Architecture](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.13768), Xia, Lu, Zhu, Xing, Zhao, Zhang, arXiv 2411.13768, 2024-2025. From a multivocal literature review, proposes evaluation-driven development and operations (EDDOps): offline and online evaluation in a closed feedback loop, building on the iterative principles of TDD and BDD for LLM agents. *Relation to this study:* Names the practice visible in several cohort repos that commit their own eval harnesses and results, including null results.

**Churn and software evolution**

- [Use of Relative Code Churn Measures to Predict System Defect Density](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/?p=151819), Nagappan, Ball, ICSE 2005. On Windows Server 2003, relative churn measures predicted defect density (89% accuracy separating fault-prone binaries); absolute churn did not. *Relation to this study:* Why we report churn relative to code size, and why high churn is a known risk signal rather than a sign of success.
- [Towards a Better Understanding of Software Evolution: An Empirical Study on Open-Source Software](https://www.cs.kent.edu/~jmaletic/cs63902/Papers/jsme11neamtiu.pdf), Neamtiu, Xie, Chen, Journal of Software: Evolution and Process, 2013. Across nine open-source projects, only Lehman's laws of continuing change and continuing growth held for every program. *Relation to this study:* Change is normal in living software, which is why we compare the cohort with famous pre-AI projects at the same age instead of judging churn in the abstract.

**Popularity signals**

- [Six Million (Suspected) Fake Stars in GitHub: A Growing Spiral of Popularity Contests, Spams, and Malware](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.13459), He, Yang, Burckhardt, Kapravelos, Vasilescu, Kästner, ICSE 2026. 6.0M suspected fake stars across 18,617 repositories (July 2019-December 2024); fake stars give only a short-lived boost (less than two months) and become a liability in the long term. *Relation to this study:* Stars are our popularity measure and a noisy one; we use them to select famous projects, not as evidence of quality.
